Foundations

How people perceive, decide, and touch · ch. 01–07

01

Working Posture

How every design question gets approached before any pixel moves

Principles are worthless without a posture — a default stance that decides what to reach for first. These four govern everything below.

☝

Mobile-first instincts

Thumb reach, one-handed use, and platform conventions come before layout aesthetics. If it fails on a phone held in one hand on a moving train, it fails.

→

Opinionated, not neutral

A recommendation with reasoning beats a survey of options. “It depends” is only acceptable when immediately followed by what it depends on and which way to lean.

§

Grounded in the classics

Nielsen, Fitts, Hick, gestalt — applied pragmatically, not recited. Theory earns its place only when it changes a decision.

⇄

Honest about trade-offs

Every design choice buys something and pays for it. Name both sides. A critique that lists only benefits is marketing, not design.

02

Laws of Interaction

The physics of interfaces — they apply whether you believe in them or not

2.1

Fitts’s Law

Time to acquire a target is a function of distance to and size of the target. Small, far-away targets are slow and error-prone.

Primary actions get big targets near the thumb or cursor rest position. Screen edges and corners are effectively infinite-size targets on desktop — use them.

2.2

Hick’s Law

Decision time grows with the number and complexity of choices. Every added option taxes every user, every time.

One primary action per screen. Cut, bury, or stage everything else via progressive disclosure. A menu of 5 beats a menu of 12, even for power users.

2.3

Jakob’s Law

Users spend most of their time in other apps. They arrive with expectations formed elsewhere and your interface inherits them.

Deviate from convention only where your product’s core differentiation lives. Spend novelty budget on the one thing that matters; keep everything else boring.

2.4

Miller’s Limit

Working memory holds roughly 4–7 chunks. Interfaces that require remembering state across screens are leaking cognition.

Recognition over recall: show the selected plan, the applied filter, the cart total. Never make the user carry information from one screen to the next in their head.

2.5

Tesler’s Law

Every process has irreducible complexity. It can be moved — to the user or into the system — but not deleted.

Absorb complexity into defaults, smart detection, and sensible presets. Ask the user only what the system genuinely cannot know.

2.6

Peak–End Rule

Experiences are judged by their most intense moment and their ending — not the average.

Polish the success state, the confirmation, the empty state after completing a task. A rough middle is forgivable; a rough ending is remembered.

03

Nielsen’s Ten, Condensed

The evaluation checklist — every screen answers to these

3.1

Visibility of system status

Every action gets feedback within 100 ms. Loading states, progress, confirmation. Silence is a bug.

3.2

Match the real world

Speak the user’s vocabulary, not the schema’s. “Trip” not “itinerary_record”.

3.3

User control & freedom

Undo beats confirmation dialogs. Every wizard needs an exit. Every destructive action needs a way back.

3.4

Consistency & standards

Internal consistency first, platform convention second, industry pattern third. Break ties in that order.

3.5

Error prevention

Disable the invalid, constrain the input, confirm the irreversible. Design so the error can’t happen, then handle it anyway.

3.6

Recognition over recall

Show options, history, and context inline. The interface remembers so the user doesn’t have to.

3.7

Flexibility & efficiency

Accelerators for experts — shortcuts, gestures, recents — that stay invisible to novices.

3.8

Aesthetic & minimalist design

Every element competes with every other element. If it doesn’t serve the task, it costs the task.

3.9

Help users recover from errors

Error messages state what happened, why, and the next step — in plain language, near the problem.

3.10

Help & documentation

The best help is contextual and just-in-time. If users need the manual, the screen has already failed once.

04

Gestalt Principles

How the eye groups before the mind reads

Users perceive structure before they read a single word. Spacing, alignment, and enclosure are doing information architecture whether you intend it or not.

Proximity
Similarity
Common region
Continuity
Figure / ground

4.1

Proximity

Whitespace is the strongest grouping tool you have. Related items sit closer together than unrelated ones — labels hug their fields, not the field above.

4.2

Similarity

Things that look alike are assumed to behave alike. If two buttons look the same, they’d better be the same weight of action.

4.3

Common region

A border or background groups more strongly than proximity. Use cards sparingly — every enclosure adds visual weight and fragments scanning.

4.4

Continuity & alignment

The eye follows lines. A single strong left-alignment edge scans faster than centered fragments. Break alignment only to signal a break in meaning.

4.5

Figure / ground

Contrast and elevation decide what reads as content and what reads as chrome. Modals, sheets, and scrims work by manipulating this deliberately.

4.6

Closure

A partially visible card at the screen edge is the strongest scroll affordance there is — stronger than any arrow or dot indicator.

05

Visual Hierarchy

The order the eye receives things is a design decision — make it on purpose

Every screen is read in some order. Hierarchy is choosing that order: what is seen first, second, third — and what is barely seen at all. When everything is emphasized, nothing is; emphasis is a zero-sum budget.

Flat — five things shout equally
Hierarchy — 1 what · 2 cost · 3 act
5.1

Size

The largest element is read first — no exceptions. A type scale exists to make this cheap and consistent.

One hero size per screen. The platform scales encode this already: Large Title 34pt over Body 17pt on iOS; Headline L 32sp over Body L 16sp in Material. If two elements tie for biggest, the screen has no first sentence.

5.2

Weight & contrast

Bold and high-contrast advances; light and low-contrast recedes. This lever is stronger than size at equal sizes.

De-emphasize with gray before you shrink — a 13pt secondary-gray label beats a 10pt black one for both hierarchy and legibility. That’s exactly what secondaryLabel (iOS) and onSurfaceVariant (M3) are for.

5.3

Space

Whitespace confers importance — margin is a spotlight. Crowding levels everything to the same rank.

Give the primary element roughly twice the breathing room of anything secondary. Spacing is also grouping (proximity, 4.1), so it does hierarchy and structure with one move.

5.4

Position

Scanning starts top-leading and follows an F or Z path; the thumb finishes at the bottom.

Message at the top, action at the bottom. First position in any list is the cheapest emphasis available — spend it on the most likely choice, not the alphabetically first one.

5.5

Color — the accent budget

On a restrained canvas, the accent color means “this matters, this is interactive.” Spend it like money.

Tint the one primary action and the key datum — nothing decorative. Both platforms enforce this: HIG wants monochromatic bars with a single tinted action; M3 routes emphasis through the primary role. This page does it too — one ice-blue, everywhere it counts.

5.6

The squint test

The verification step: squint at the screen (or blur a screenshot) until the words disappear.

Three levels should survive the blur: one hero, a support layer, and a whisper of detail. If the primary action doesn’t survive, the hierarchy failed — no amount of polish rescues it.

Same rule for actions — one filled · one outlined · text for the rest
06

Mobile Instincts

The phone is held, not sat at — design for the hand first

Roughly half of phone use is one-handed. The bottom half of the screen belongs to the thumb; the top corners are hostile territory reserved for rare actions.

Hard · rare actions Stretch · secondary Natural · primary

Rules of the hand

  • Primary actions live at the bottom. Tab bars, FABs, and sticky CTAs exist because of this map.
  • Destructive actions go out of easy reach — or behind a deliberate gesture. Reachability cuts both ways.
  • Top-left back button is a stretch. Support the edge-swipe gesture; never make the corner the only way home.
  • Reachability degrades with screen size. What was “natural” on 5.4″ is “stretch” on 6.7″.

6.1

Progressive disclosure over density

Show the 20% that serves 80% of visits; stage the rest behind clear, honest entry points. Density is a desktop luxury.

6.2

Gestures need visible fallbacks

Swipe-to-delete, pull-to-refresh, long-press: fine as accelerators, never as the only path. Undiscoverable ≠ elegant.

6.3

Respect the keyboard

The keyboard eats half the screen. Keep the focused field and the submit action visible above it; use the right keyboard type per field.

6.4

Design the interruptions

Mobile sessions are 30-second fragments. Preserve state ruthlessly — a form that loses input to a phone call has failed.

07

Accessibility

Not a compliance checkbox — part of the definition of done

Roughly one person in four has a disability, and everyone is situationally impaired daily — sunlight on the screen, one occupied hand, a loud train, tired eyes. Accessible design isn’t a separate discipline; it’s the same design, done thoroughly. And because both platforms build it into their components, most of it is free — until you draw something custom.

The page grading its own inks — WCAG: ≥4.5:1 body · ≥3:1 large text & UI parts
7.1

Contrast is arithmetic, not taste

WCAG minimums: 4.5:1 for body text, 3:1 for large text (≥18pt, or 14pt bold) and essential UI parts. Aim for 7:1 where reading is sustained.

Measure, don’t eyeball — dark themes fail more often than light ones because desaturated gray-on-graphite feels tasteful at 2.8:1. Check both appearances; a color that passes in light mode can fail in dark.

7.2

Type must scale — layout is the real test

Dynamic Type (iOS) and sp units (Android) mean users choose their text size, up to accessibility sizes ~3× the default. The font scales automatically; your layout is what breaks.

Use the platform text styles and sp everywhere, then test at the largest AX size: rows must grow, side-by-side must stack, and nothing may truncate. A label that ellipsizes at AX3 is a layout bug, not a copy problem.

7.3

The screen reader hears your hierarchy

VoiceOver and TalkBack turn every screen into a sequence: label, role, value, hint. If that sequence is chaos, the visual hierarchy usually is too — the two audits find each other’s bugs.

Every control gets a real label (“Delete invoice”, not “trash”). Group related elements so a card reads as one stop, not seven. Hide decorative images. Then run your core flow eyes-closed — it’s the cheapest expert review you’ll ever do.

7.4

Color never carries meaning alone

About 1 in 12 men has a color-vision deficiency; every user loses color fidelity in sunlight. Red-vs-green as the only signal is invisible to millions.

Pair color with a second channel — icon, label, weight, position. An error is a red border plus an icon plus a message. Verify by viewing the screen in grayscale; the meaning should survive.

7.5

Motion is optional

Parallax, zooms, and springs can trigger vestibular disorders. Both platforms expose a Reduce Motion setting; honoring it is not negotiable.

Respect prefers-reduced-motion (this page does), accessibilityReduceMotion in SwiftUI, and the animator duration scale on Android. The fallback is a cross-fade — never “no feedback at all.”

7.6

Focus is navigation too

Hardware keyboards on iPads, switch control, full keyboard access, and every web user who tabs: focus order is a first-class navigation path.

Focus order follows visual order; the focus indicator stays visible (never outline: none without a replacement); modals trap focus and return it on dismiss. If you can’t complete the core flow with a keyboard, it isn’t done.

7.7

Built-ins are accessible by default

System components ship with labels, roles, focus behavior, Dynamic Type, and contrast-checked colors. Every custom control re-earns all of it by hand — or silently ships without it.

This is the accessibility case for chapters 15–16: restyle built-ins rather than rebuild them, and put accessibility in the definition of done for any custom component. Retrofits cost multiples of doing it now.

II

Method

From idea to iteration — how the work gets done · ch. 08–10

08

The Process

From app idea to shipped design — in order, no skipping

Screens are the last thing you draw, not the first. This sequence front-loads the decisions that are expensive to change (what it is, how it’s organized) and defers the ones that are cheap (colors, corner radii). Chapter 29 runs the whole sequence on one app idea, end to end.

8.1

Frame the product

Who is it for, what job does it do, and what is the one thing it must do brilliantly. One sentence, written down.

“For [person] in [situation], this app [does job] — and it is brilliant at [one thing].” Every later design dispute gets resolved against this sentence.

8.2

Map the flows

The top three user journeys as task flows — before any screen exists.

Boxes are user intentions (“capture expense”, “find last invoice”), not screens. Count the steps in the most frequent flow; that number is your budget to beat.

8.3

Choose the information architecture

What lives where, and which navigation model carries it. The most expensive decision in the whole process.

2–5 peer destinations → tab/nav bar. One dominant feed plus actions → single screen with modals. Deep hierarchy → drill-down list. If you can’t name a tab in one word, it isn’t a tab.

8.4

Wireframe the core loop

Low fidelity, grayscale, one platform-neutral pass over the 20% of screens that serve 80% of sessions.

Real content, never lorem ipsum — layout decisions made against fake content are fake decisions. If the hierarchy needs color to work, the hierarchy is broken.

8.5

Split into dialects

One shared core — IA, flows, vocabulary, brand — expressed through two idiomatic layers: HIG for iOS, Material 3 for Android.

The user should recognize the app across platforms but never feel it was ported. Chapter 11 is the mapping table.

8.6

Design the edges

Empty, loading, error, offline, interrupted, first-run. The happy path is a minority of real sessions.

The empty state is the first screen most users ever meet — make it teach. An error state that only says “something went wrong” is a design task abandoned, not completed.

8.7

Audit, test, iterate

Run the screen audit (ch. 28), the thumb map (ch. 06), and put it in front of real people.

Five users surface most usability problems — test the wireframe, not the polished mockup, so findings are cheap to act on. Then loop back to 8.4.

09

Four Modes of Work

Every design engagement is one of these — know which one you’re in

Misdiagnosing the mode wastes the session: you can’t wireframe your way out of a strategy question, and a philosophical debate won’t fix a broken checkout. Name the mode, then run its playbook.

◎

Critique a project

A screen, flow, or product exists and needs expert eyes.

  1. Ask the goal and audience before judging anything
  2. Five-second test: what reads first, what’s the assumed action?
  3. Walk Nielsen’s ten (ch. 03) against each screen
  4. Thumb map + platform dialect audit (ch. 06, 11)
  5. Rank findings by severity × effort to fix

Every criticism ships with a concrete recommendation.

⌖

Diagnose a problem

Something feels off — confusion, drop-off, low conversion.

  1. Locate the drop-off precisely: which step, which screen
  2. Hypothesize against the laws — choice overload (Hick)? missing feedback (3.1)? recall burden (2.4)?
  3. Watch three real sessions before changing anything
  4. Fix the smallest plausible cause first; measure; repeat

Symptoms lie; funnels and session recordings don’t.

§

Reason a decision

“Should we do X or Y?” — a principle question in disguise.

  1. Name the trade-off axis it sits on (ch. 27)
  2. Find the governing law or heuristic
  3. Check three best-in-class apps — Jakob’s law sets expectations
  4. Decide, and write the reasoning down

A decision without written reasoning gets relitigated forever.

✎

Produce artifacts

Wireframes, mockups, prototypes — something to look at and test.

  1. Match fidelity to the question being asked
  2. Sketch answers flow · wireframe answers layout · mockup answers style · prototype answers feel
  3. Real content from the first sketch
  4. Derive both dialects from the shared core (8.5)

Never polish what hasn’t been validated one fidelity below.

10

Research & Testing

Opinions are hypotheses — here’s how they get tested

The process says “put it in front of real people” (8.7); this chapter is how. The method matters more than the sample size: a badly run test with twenty users produces confident nonsense, while five users and a disciplined script find most of what’s broken.

The evidence ladder — trust flows downward; never ship on the top rung alone
10.1

Test tasks, not opinions

“Do you like it?” produces politeness. “Book the cheapest room for Friday” produces behavior — and behavior is the only honest witness.

Write 3–5 tasks from the top flows (8.2), phrased as goals, never as UI directions: “find out when your plant needs water,” not “tap the Today tab.” Naming a UI element in the task erases the finding it was meant to produce.

10.2

Don’t lead the witness

The moderator’s job is silence. Every hint, every “what do you think that button does?”, every rescued participant destroys the data they were recruited to provide.

Ask them to think aloud, then say nothing. When they stall, the answer to “what should I do?” is “what would you do if I weren’t here?” Let them fail — the failure is the finding. Debrief questions come after all tasks, never during.

10.3

Five users, then fix, then five more

Five users surface most usability problems of the version they saw. The power isn’t the number — it’s the iteration: test, fix, retest beats one large study every time.

Budget three rounds of five over one round of fifteen. Test the wireframe (8.4) first, when findings cost minutes to act on. Stop recruiting when sessions stop surprising you.

10.4

Severity beats vote-counting

“Two of five users struggled” is not a statistic — the sample is too small for math. Findings are judged by severity: does it block the task, slow it, or merely annoy?

Rate each finding blocker / friction / cosmetic, tied to the flow it damages. One user hitting a blocker on the core loop outranks four users disliking a color. This feeds straight into the critique ranking (Mode 1, 9).

10.5

A/B tests answer “which,” not “why”

Production experiments are the bottom rung of the ladder — the strongest evidence, but only for comparing variants you already believed in. They can’t generate the insight that produces the variants.

Usability tests find the problem; A/B tests confirm the fix at scale. Don’t A/B what a five-user test settles in a day, and don’t trust a test without a success metric named before it runs (guardrails too — conversion up while retention quietly dies is a loss).

10.6

Analytics is the always-on usability test

Funnels, drop-off points, rage taps, and dead-end screens are behavioral findings arriving continuously — Mode 2 (9) runs on them.

Instrument the flows you mapped in 8.2 — step by step, not just endpoints — so “where do we lose people?” has an answer before anyone asks. Then watch three real session recordings before believing any hypothesis; numbers locate, sessions explain.

11.1

The iOS accent, in one breath

Controls float above content on Liquid Glass; the content owns the screen.

No custom bar backgrounds or tints — use the scroll edge effect. Monochromatic bar icons, one tinted prominent action. Large titles that collapse on scroll. Sheets with medium/large detents for scoped tasks. Semantic colors, both appearances, always.

11.2

The Android accent, in one breath

Build on tokens — color roles, type scale, shape scale — so the app inherits Material You personalization for free.

Nav bar → rail as the window widens. The FAB carries the action. Bottom sheets over menus on compact. Predictive back everywhere. Save expressive moves — springs, shape morphs, emphasized type — for one or two hero moments.

11.3

Desktop is not a big phone

Density, hover, multi-window, drag-and-drop, and keyboard-first flows are desktop’s native strengths. A stretched mobile layout on a 27″ display wastes the medium.

11.4

Web is the ambiguous middle

Assume both touch and cursor. Deep-linkable URLs, working browser back, and responsive reflow are the web’s conventions — break them and the platform breaks you.

12

Navigation

Where am I · how do I get back · where else can I go

Navigation is the information architecture made visible — and it’s where iOS and Android differ most deeply, because they answer “back” differently at the operating-system level. Users never read a sitemap; they build a mental map from movement. Every screen must answer three questions without demanding memory: where am I, how do I get back, where else can I go.

12.1

The three questions, answered on-screen

Where am I (a title and a lit-up tab), how do I get back (a visible affordance plus the system gesture), where else can I go (persistent destinations). Any answer that lives in the user’s memory instead of on the screen is a defect (3.6).

Audit by screenshot: hand someone any single screen cold. If they can’t answer all three in five seconds, navigation has failed — no matter how elegant the transitions are.

12.2

The content’s shape picks the model

Flat peer sections → tab/nav bar. A deep catalog → drill-down stack. One dominant workspace with occasional tasks → hub plus modals. A linear commitment (checkout, onboarding) → pipeline with progress.

Most real apps compose two: tabs at the root, stacks within each tab, modals on top. If you’re inventing a fourth layer, the IA (8.3) is overloaded — restructure the content, don’t decorate the navigation.

12.3

Back is sacred — and means different things

Android has one universal back: a system gesture that works everywhere, now with a predictive preview of where it lands. iOS has contextual backs: edge-swipe and chevron on stacks, swipe-down on sheets — each surface carries its own exit.

Never intercept, repurpose, or “are you sure?” the back gesture; guard unsaved work at the moment of loss, not the moment of leaving. On Android, support predictive back rather than fighting it; on iOS, every modal needs a visible dismiss and the gesture (26 ✕).

12.4

Tabs are places, not steps

A tab is a room, not a doorway in a corridor. Each keeps its own stack and scroll position; switching away and back must return the user exactly where they were.

Preserve per-tab state on both platforms — losing a half-read thread because someone glanced at another tab is state destruction (6.4). The one sanctioned exception: re-tapping the current tab scrolls to top or pops to root.

12.5

Modality is a promise

A push says “you’re traveling deeper.” A modal says “brief detour — you’ll return to exactly where you were.” Breaking that promise (modals that navigate away, sheets hiding whole hierarchies) breaks the user’s map.

Modal for scoped tasks with a clear end — compose, edit, pick, pay. Push for browsing. A modal whose internal stack runs more than two screens deep wanted to be a push (26 ✕: app-within-app).

12.6

Deep links define the address space

Notifications, widgets, share sheets, and system search all land people mid-hierarchy. Navigation state is data: every screen needs an address, and arriving at one must still answer “how do I get back?”

Synthesize the up-stack on Android (back from a deep-linked screen walks the hierarchy, not out of the app); restore surrounding context on iOS. If a screen can’t be deep-linked, its place in the IA was never actually decided.

12.7

Don’t invent navigation

Radial menus, gesture-only spatial canvases, hamburger-hidden everything: novel navigation spends the entire novelty budget (2.3) on the one thing users need to be boring.

Users learned tabs, stacks, and sheets from every other app on their phone. Spend creativity inside the rooms — never on the corridors between them. If the product’s differentiation genuinely is navigation (maps, canvases, games), that’s a deliberate ch. 27 trade, made with eyes open.

12.8

Search is teleportation — treat it as navigation

Search bypasses the hierarchy entirely, which makes it the pressure valve for deep IA — and a navigation surface with its own rules, not a text field with results.

The zero-state shows recents and suggestions before a character is typed (3.6); results land like deep links — with a working way back and the query preserved for refinement. Placement is a dialect choice (11): a search tab or bottom-toolbar field on iOS, the morphing search bar on Android. If analytics show search carrying your main flows, the hierarchy is failing — fix the IA, don’t just polish the escape hatch.

13

Adaptive Layout

Tablets, foldables, split screens — design for size classes, not devices

A tablet is not a big phone (11.3’s cousin: a stretched phone layout on a 13″ screen wastes the medium twice — empty acres and unreachable controls). Both platforms hand you the same abstraction: the window’s size class, not the device’s name, decides the layout.

One app, three window classes — navigation and panes morph, content and IA never do
13.1

Size classes, not devices

Material’s window classes (compact <600dp, medium, expanded ≥840dp) and iOS’s horizontal compact/regular describe the window — and windows change: rotation, split screen, Stage Manager, a foldable unfolding.

Design one layout per class, not per device. A phone in landscape video, an iPad in one-third Split View, and a folded flip phone are all “compact” — if the layout only asks “is this an iPad?”, it’s already wrong somewhere.

13.2

Spend width on structure, not stretching

Wider windows buy panes, not longer lines. The canonical layouts — list-detail, supporting pane, feed grid — are the honest ways to spend the space.

Cap text measure (~70ch) and let panes absorb the rest: master list + detail, content + inspector, feed at higher column count. A form that grows 900dp-wide inputs didn’t adapt; it inflated (18.2 still applies).

13.3

List–detail has rules

The pattern everyone reaches for, with the details everyone forgets: selection must be visibly persistent, the empty detail pane must say something, and back must mean the right thing in each class.

On expanded, highlight the selected row and give the unselected detail pane a purpose (“Select a plant”). On compact, the same navigation becomes push-and-back. NavigationSplitView and ListDetailPaneScaffold both encode this — let them (15).

13.4

The thumb map doesn’t transfer

Tablets are held by the edges with two hands — now the corners and edges are reachable and the bottom-center is far. The phone’s thumb zones (06) invert.

Primary actions move toward the leading/trailing edges and top bar; two-hand symmetric layouts (rail left, actions right) beat centered bottom bars. Test holding the tablet, not pointing at it on a desk.

13.5

Foldables fold; state must not

Unfolding mid-task is a live window resize. Tabletop and book postures add a hinge that content must respect — and everything in 6.4 (state survives interruption) applies at double intensity.

Scroll position, form input, selection, and media playback all survive the fold/unfold transition. In tabletop posture, split content above the hinge and controls below; never place a tap target on the crease.

13.6

Big screens attract other inputs

Tablets come with keyboards, trackpads, and styluses attached. The moment a window widens, assume a pointer might arrive with it.

Hover states, keyboard shortcuts (⌘-based on iPad), focus order (7.6), and right-click/secondary-click menus stop being desktop luxuries at medium width and up. A tablet app with no shortcuts feels like a kiosk.

14

Iconography & Imagery

Icons are vocabulary, images are evidence — neither is decoration

Icons are the interface’s shared vocabulary, and each platform publishes its own dictionary: SF Symbols on iOS, Material Symbols on Android. Using them isn’t laziness — it’s speaking words users already know, drawn on metrics the system text already uses.

Two dictionaries, one shared grammar — outline rests, fill selects
14.1

Use the system dictionary first

Thousands of symbols, drawn on the platform’s text metrics, scaling with Dynamic Type, animating for free (SF Symbols), or varying along weight/fill/grade axes as a variable font (Material Symbols).

Reach for a custom glyph only when the domain concept genuinely isn’t in the library — then draw it on the same grid, at the same weights, so it doesn’t read as a foreign word. Test custom icons at the largest Dynamic Type size the bar will meet.

14.2

Icons label, or icons lie

Perhaps a dozen symbols are truly universal — search, back, share, settings, play. Everything else is a guess the user makes on your behalf (26 ✕: mystery meat).

Pair icons with text labels in tab bars and nav bars, always. An icon-only toolbar is acceptable only when every glyph is in the universal dozen — and even then, accessibility labels are non-negotiable (7.3).

14.3

The fill convention is grammar

Outline means resting; fill means selected or active. Both platforms teach this in their tab bars, so users read it without thinking — until an app breaks the rule and every state becomes a riddle.

Use the outline↔fill pair of the same symbol for state, never two different symbols. And don’t invert the rule for style — a filled resting state reads as “already on.”

14.4

The app icon is the smallest billboard

It’s the brand at 60px, judged in a grid of rivals, re-tinted by the OS: iOS renders layered icons in default/dark/clear/tinted modes (Icon Composer); Android’s themed icons take Material You’s wallpaper tint via a monochrome layer.

One bold shape, no words, no photos, no screenshots of the UI (Apple’s own don’ts). Design the layers and the monochrome variant from day one — an icon that ignores dark and tinted modes looks abandoned on a 2026 home screen.

14.5

Photos are evidence, illustration is explanation

Photography asserts a specific reality — the user’s actual plant, the actual product for sale. Illustration explains concepts, softens empty states, and carries brand where there’s nothing yet to show.

Match the medium to the claim: never stock photos where users expect their own data, never abstract illustration where a real photo would answer the question. Decoration that pushes content below the fold is paying rent with the user’s time.

14.6

Image discipline

Images are the heaviest, slowest, most layout-hostile content on any screen — treat them as a managed material.

Fixed aspect-ratio containers so nothing jumps when they arrive (20.3); meaningful images get real accessibility descriptions and decorative ones get hidden (7.3); check every image against dark mode — a white-matte logo on graphite is the classic miss.

14.7

The two dictionaries barely overlap — and that’s fine

SF Symbols runs past 7,000 glyphs; Material Symbols sits around 2,500–3,800 depending on how the variable-axis styles are counted. Only a few hundred are formally mapped one-to-one between them — cross-platform tooling like Expo’s symbol system ships roughly 570 confirmed pairs. That’s a small, concentrated core inside two much larger, mostly disjoint libraries.

A small mapped core inside two mostly-disjoint libraries — depth diverges by design

For the dozen truly universal actions the metaphor usually does converge: magnifying glass, gear, trash can, heart, star, house, checkmark all read the same on both platforms — safe to treat as shared vocabulary. Past that dozen, don’t assume a match exists; look it up per icon rather than porting a Material glyph into an iOS toolbar or vice versa.

14.8

Same meaning, different glyph: share is the tell

iOS draws share as a box with an arrow escaping upward — an object leaving through an opening, tied to the Action-sheet model since iOS 7. Android draws three connected dots forming a triangle with radiating lines — a one-to-many broadcast, inherited from a 2006 browser plugin. Same user intent, genuinely different mental model, not just a style difference.

This is the load-bearing example for chapter 11 (One Design, Two Dialects): render the platform’s own share glyph, never the other one. Recognition testing on the Android icon alone has landed around half — importing it into an iOS screen would cost that recognition twice over. When in doubt, ship the native symbol and let the dialect (11) do its job.

Systems & Craft

The details that decide whether it ships well · ch. 16–25

16

Design Systems

Decisions made once, so screens don’t relitigate them

A design system is the product’s design memory: every choice that shouldn’t be re-made per screen — captured as tokens, components, patterns, and the rules for using them. Its value isn’t beauty; it’s that a hundred screens stay coherent while ten people ship them.

Change flows upward — fix the token, and every layer above inherits it
16.1

A system is rules, not a UI kit

A Figma library without usage guidance is a parts bin. The system is the components plus when-to-use, when-not-to, and who decides.

Every component ships with its anti-patterns: “segmented control — 2–5 options, never 1” is the documentation format. If a component needs a meeting to use correctly, the docs failed.

16.2

Start from the platform’s system

HIG components and Material 3 are mature, accessible, user-tested design systems you get for free — with dark mode, Dynamic Type, and predictive back already solved.

Wrap and theme system components; rebuild only where the product’s differentiation lives (Jakob’s law economics, 2.3). Every custom control is a maintenance contract with no expiry date.

16.3

Detach rate is the health metric

When designers keep overriding a component or spawning one-off variants, that isn’t indiscipline — it’s the system telling you a decision is wrong or missing.

Audit the one-offs quarterly. Three teams hacking the same card variant means the system owes them that variant; promote it, don’t police them.

16.4

One system, two dialects

The system mirrors chapter 11: a shared semantic layer (tokens, naming, patterns) with per-platform component implementations.

One token sheet feeds both platforms; “primary action” resolves to a prominent toolbar button on iOS and a FAB or filled button on Android. Share the decision, not the pixels.

16.5

Governance or decay — pick one

A system nobody can change becomes a cage; a system anybody can change becomes noise. Both end with teams ignoring it.

Name an owner. Provide a lightweight contribution path (propose → review → promote). Version it, deprecate loudly, and never break existing screens silently.

16.6

Every setting is a decision you refused to make

A preference toggle is the design team outsourcing a choice to thousands of people less equipped to make it. Some settings are legitimate — accessibility, notification volume, genuinely divergent contexts. Most are surrender.

Before adding a setting, exhaust the alternatives: a better default (18.6), smart detection, or simply deciding. When one survives, organize by user goal (“Notifications”, “Privacy”), never by engineering module — and let the app follow the system (theme, text size) instead of duplicating OS settings inside it (26 ✕).

16.7

Privacy is a design decision, not a legal one

Consent is a moment the user actually experiences, not a checkbox legal signed off on. Treated as a system-level default — like tokens (17) or settings (16.6) — it stays consistent instead of being reinvented, and re-weakened, screen by screen.

Ask for data at the point of use, not at install (22.2); state what it’s for in the same sentence as the ask; default every optional collection to off. One honest permission screen beats a privacy policy nobody reads — and “Ask App Not to Track” worded as a trap is the dark-pattern line this manual already refuses to cross (26 ✕).

17

Design Tokens

Name the decision, not the value — the value will change

A token is a named design decision: color.action.primary, not #4cc2ff. The name captures intent; the value is an implementation detail that can be swapped per theme, platform, or brand without touching a single screen.

Components reference semantic tokens — never primitives directly
17.1

Three tiers, strict direction

Primitives hold raw values (blue-500, space-4). Semantic tokens give them meaning (color.accent, space.inset.card). Component tokens bind them to parts (button.fill).

References flow one way: component → semantic → primitive. A screen that reaches past the semantic layer to grab blue-500 has re-made a decision the system already made.

17.2

Name by role, never by value

If the name would be wrong after the value changes, the name is wrong now. accent survives a rebrand; blue doesn’t.

The platforms are already role-named token systems — secondaryLabel and semantic colors on iOS, primary/onSurfaceVariant color roles and the type scale in M3. Map your brand onto those roles rather than inventing a parallel vocabulary.

17.3

Tokens make theming free

Dark mode, Material You dynamic color, high-contrast, density, white-labeling — each is just a different value set behind the same names.

This page is the demo: it went paper-and-vermilion → carbon → graphite-and-ice-blue by editing a handful of CSS variables, zero markup changes. If a retheme requires touching screens, tokens were bypassed somewhere.

17.4

Tokenize more than color

Type styles, the spacing scale, corner radii, elevation, motion durations and springs, z-order — anything decided more than once deserves a name.

Spacing on a 4pt/4dp base scale; radii mapped to the platform shape scale (M3: none → full); motion as tokens (M3’s spatial/effects springs, fast/default/slow) so “how things move” is one decision, not fifty.

18

Forms & Input

Where most apps actually lose people

A form is a conversation where one side does all the work. Every field is a demand on the user’s patience, memory, and thumbs — and checkout, sign-up, and onboarding funnels leak more users at forms than anywhere else. Design them like they cost money, because they do.

Anatomy of a field — input preserved, problem named, fix stated
18.1

Ask less

Every field costs completions. Derive what you can — card type from its number, city from the postcode — and treat “optional” as a synonym for “cut it.”

Fight for each field’s removal; make the requester prove its value in data. If the business genuinely needs it, stage it for later (progressive profiling) instead of taxing the first session.

18.2

One column, labels on top

Multi-column forms break the scan line (4.4) and get skipped fields; placeholder-as-label vanishes the moment typing starts — a recall burden mid-task (2.4).

Single column, related fields grouped (4.1), label above the field, placeholder reserved for format examples only. The eye should travel one straight line from first field to submit.

18.3

The right keyboard, the right autofill

An email field that opens the default keyboard, or a code field that blocks paste, is self-inflicted friction.

Set keyboardType + textContentType (iOS) / KeyboardOptions + autofill hints (Android) on every field — that’s what makes one-tap credential, address, and OTP autofill work. Test the whole form with a password manager before shipping.

18.4

Validate at the right moment

Keystroke validation yells at half-typed input; submit-only validation ambushes at the end. Neither is a conversation.

Format checks on blur, cross-field checks on submit, and quiet positive ticks on hard fields (IBAN, password rules). Keep submit enabled — let the tap point precisely at what’s missing rather than leaving a dead button to decode (26 ✕).

18.5

Errors are directions, not verdicts

An error state has one job: get the user to the fixed state with everything they typed intact.

Never clear the form. Name the field, the problem, and the fix, next to the field (“One digit short — card numbers have 16”), not in a toast that vanishes. “Something went wrong” is a shrug, not a message (see ch. 19).

18.6

Defaults do the work

Tesler’s law (2.5) applied: every default the system supplies is a decision the user doesn’t make.

Default to the most common answer, remember last-used choices, prefill from context (country from locale, date from today) — and never pre-select anything destructive, consent-granting, or paid.

18.7

Pick the control by its answer shape

Checkbox, radio, toggle, stepper, slider, and picker aren’t interchangeable skins on the same idea — each implies a different question, and using the wrong one makes the user translate before they can answer.

Radio: one of a few, always visible, choice matters now (plan tier). Checkbox: any number, independent (toppings). Toggle: a binary system state that applies immediately, no submit (Wi-Fi). Stepper: a small, precise count (party size). Slider: an approximate value in a range where the exact number matters less than the feel (brightness) — never for a number someone might type instead. Picker: one from many, especially dates. A toggle that needs a “Save” button is lying about being a toggle.

Every label, button, and error is interface. Words are the cheapest material in the product to change and the most expensive to get wrong — a vague button costs more taps than a misaligned one.

Speak the platform’s casing

20

Empty & Loading States

The screens you didn’t design are the ones users remember

The full-data happy path in every mockup is the rarest screen in production. First launch is empty. Every fetch starts loading. Networks drop. Step 8.6 named these edges; this chapter designs the two most common — because “blank” and “waiting” are where trust is won or quietly lost.

The response-time ladder — match the feedback to the wait
20.1

Empty states come in species

First-run empty (nothing exists yet), success-empty (the user cleared it), and no-results (a search or filter found nothing). One generic “Nothing here” fails all three.

First-run teaches: what will live here, plus the first action (19.4). Success-empty celebrates: “All watered — see you Thursday.” No-results redirects: echo the query, offer the loosened filter. Each species gets its own copy and its own call to action.

20.2

No-results is a fork, not a wall

An empty search result is the system saying “try differently” — so say how. A dead-end here sends the user to a competitor with a better shrug.

Show what was searched, name the active filters (they’re the usual culprit), and make “clear filters” one tap. If nearby or related matches exist, show them under an honest label — never silently substitute.

20.3

Skeletons over spinners

A spinner measures the delay; a skeleton promises the structure. Skeletons read as faster because the layout arrives instantly and the content merely fills in.

Mirror the real layout exactly — a skeleton that doesn’t match causes the reflow it exists to prevent. Content must never jump under a poised finger: reserve space for images, and never let late content move a button someone is about to tap.

20.4

Don’t flash, don’t strobe

A spinner that appears for 80ms is visual noise; five per screen is a seizure of uncertainty. Loading indication has its own hierarchy (ch. 05).

Delay indicators ~200–300ms so fast loads show nothing at all. One indicator per surface — skeleton the whole region, don’t spin every card. For waits under 5s on Android, M3’s shape-morphing LoadingIndicator is the idiom; keep spinners for genuinely unknown waits.

20.5

Optimistic UI, honest rollback

For actions that almost always succeed — like, rename, mark watered — act as if they succeeded instantly and reconcile in the background. Waiting for the server to permit a heart animation is borrowed sluggishness.

Sprout marks a plant watered on tap (29.5): instant tick, haptic, snackbar-undo on Android. On failure, revert visibly and say why. Reserve blocking, pessimistic waits for money and destruction — exactly where optimism would lie.

20.6

Offline is a state, not an error

“No connection” with a sad cloud treats a normal condition — elevators, flights, dead zones — as a catastrophe.

Show cached content clearly marked as such; queue writes and sync when the network returns; distinguish “can’t load right now” from “nothing exists” — they demand opposite messages. The app should feel paused offline, never broken.

21

Motion & Feedback

Animation is information — or it’s in the way

Motion answers the questions the eye asks during a state change: where did that come from, where did it go, did my tap register? Feedback closes the loop on every action. Both platforms ship complete motion systems — the design work is deciding what each movement says, and saying nothing decoratively.

Position over time — arrivals earn attention, departures get out of the way
21.1

Motion is causality

A transition’s job is to explain the state change it accompanies: the sheet rose from the bottom (so swipe it back down), the deleted row collapsed into nothing, the card expanded into the detail screen.

For every animation, name the question it answers. “Where did this come from?” — good. “Doesn’t it feel premium?” — cut it. Container transforms and shared-element transitions are causality made visible; confetti is not.

21.2

The 100ms contract

Feedback and completion are different events. The press state, highlight, or haptic acknowledges the tap now; the result arrives whenever it arrives (20.5 handles the gap).

Every interactive element gets a visible pressed state — custom buttons included (Apple’s own “don’t”: a custom button with no press state reads as a broken screen). Acknowledge in <100ms even when the work takes seconds (3.1).

21.3

Duration scales with distance

Small elements snap (~100–200ms); full-screen surfaces take ~300–400ms; nothing in a daily-use flow should exceed that. Enters decelerate, exits accelerate — arrivals earn attention, departures get out of the way.

Use the platform’s tokens, not hand-rolled curves: SwiftUI’s springs are the default physics; M3 provides spatial/effects spring tokens in fast/default/slow. A 500ms transition feels delightful once and slow the other thousand times.

21.4

Haptics are a vocabulary, not a garnish

iOS defines meanings: notification (success/warning/error), impact (light→heavy), selection ticks. Android exposes equivalent haptic constants. Users learn these meanings across the whole OS — use them as defined.

Haptics confirm what the eye might miss: Sprout’s watering tick lands as touch, not just pixels. Never on scroll, never decoratively, never as the only channel. System controls already play their own — don’t double-fire.

21.5

One hero moment

Expressive motion is a budget, like novelty (2.3) and color (5.5). M3 Expressive says it outright: bounce and shape-morphing belong to one or two hero moments; everything utilitarian runs the standard scheme.

Pick the moment that is the product — Sprout’s checkmark morph on watering, a payment success, an inbox-zero flourish — and spend the personality there (2.6, peak–end). Tab switches, list loads, and back navigations stay instant and boring, forever.

21.6

Motion is optional — still

Chapter 7.5 is law here too: Reduce Motion replaces movement with cross-fades, and no meaning may travel by motion alone.

Pair every motion cue with a state change that survives its removal — the row is checked, not just bounced. Test the app with Reduce Motion on; if anything becomes confusing, motion was carrying information it never should have carried alone.

21.7

Sound is a channel — the quietest one you have

Sound reaches users when their eyes are elsewhere, which is exactly why it must be spent like the accent color (5.5): message received, payment confirmed, timer done — moments that matter away from the screen.

Respect the silent switch and Focus modes absolutely; pair every sound with a visual and usually a haptic (never the sole channel, 7.5’s sibling); use the platform’s semantic sounds before inventing your own. An app that clicks on every tap is an app that gets muted with the whole phone.

22

Onboarding & First-Run

Most apps die in the first session — design the first five minutes hardest

The median app loses most of its new users within days, and the first session does the deciding. Onboarding is not a welcome carousel — it is the shortest designed path from install to the user’s first genuine success.

The first-session order — asks come after value, or they come at a discount
22.1

Name the aha moment, then shorten the road

Every product has one event after which people tend to stay — the first watering logged, the first message answered, the first file synced. That event, not the install, is what onboarding delivers.

Write it down next to the framing sentence (8.1), count the taps between first launch and it, and treat every one of them as a toll. Sprout’s is “first plant added, first task checked” — reachable in under a minute, no account required.

22.2

Value before identity

A sign-up wall before any value is the app demanding trust it hasn’t earned (26 ✕). Let people taste the product anonymously wherever the architecture allows.

Ask for the account when it protects something of theirs — their plants, their history, their sync — and say so in the ask: “Create an account to keep your garden safe.” Offer the platform’s one-tap sign-in so the toll is one tap, not a form (18.1).

22.3

Teach by doing, not by carousel

Three-swipe intro tours are skipped by the users who need them and forgotten by the ones who read them. Instruction that arrives before the situation it explains is noise.

The first-run empty state is the real syllabus (20.1): it says what belongs here and hands over the first action. Contextual hints appear at first contact with a feature — once, dismissible, never again (3.10).

22.4

One permission, one moment, one reason

A cold-launch stack of system dialogs is answered no in self-defense — and iOS gives you exactly one shot at each. The system dialog is the contract; your priming screen is the pitch.

Ask at the moment the feature needs it, primed with the user’s own context: “Want a nudge when Monstera is thirsty?” (29.6). If they decline the pitch, don’t burn the system dialog — leave the door open in settings.

22.5

Disclose power progressively

Advanced features shown on day one are clutter; the same features surfaced at the moment of relevance are delight (2.2, 6.1).

Gate discovery on behavior, not time: the user who adds a tenth plant gets shown bulk actions; the one who exports twice learns about automation. Feature discovery is a drip, not a firehose — and never a badge-covered “What’s New” museum.

22.6

Returning is also a first run

Reinstalls, new phones, and second devices outnumber true first-times for mature products — and nothing insults a returning user like a welcome tour.

Detect the existing account and lead with restore: “Welcome back — your 12 plants are safe.” Skip the teaching, keep the permission re-asks (a new device reset them), and never make someone rebuild what sync should have carried.

23

Notifications & Interruptions

Permission to interrupt is a loan — every pointless push is a missed repayment

A notification reaches through someone’s day, past their attention, into their pocket. That access was granted once (22.4) and is silently revoked in the settings you’ll never see. The design question is never “can we send this?” — it’s “would the user have asked for this?”

Two honest questions before every push — most events fail the first one
23.1

The trust economy

Users don’t complain about bad notifications — they mute the channel, bury the app, or delete it. Each pointless push spends permission you cannot buy back with a good one.

Track opt-out, mute, and “deliver quietly” rates per notification type as first-class health metrics, exactly like crash rates. A notification type nobody would miss is a notification type to delete.

23.2

The bar: theirs, timely, actionable

“Monstera is thirsty” clears it: about their plant, on the right day, fixable in one tap. “We miss you! 🌱” fails all three — it’s about the app’s retention curve, not the user’s life.

Every push must name something of the user’s and arrive when acting on it makes sense. Marketing goes in a channel the user explicitly opted into — piggybacking promo onto a transactional channel is a dark pattern wearing a bell.

23.3

Channels and levels are the user’s volume knob

Android notification channels let users tune each type independently; iOS interruption levels (passive → active → time-sensitive → critical) decide what pierces Focus modes. Both work only if you classify honestly.

One channel per genuine type — reminders, replies, promotions — never one bucket for everything (muting one thing shouldn’t require muting all). And never inflate levels: a sale marked time-sensitive teaches the OS, and the user, that you lie.

23.4

Group, summarize, respect the pile

Five likes are one event, not five interruptions. Both platforms thread and group; iOS adds scheduled summaries for the merely-interesting.

Group by conversation or object (“3 plants need you today”, one push), coalesce bursts server-side, and route low-urgency types to quiet delivery or a daily digest. The lock screen is shared real estate — apps that pile get evicted.

23.5

Actionable at the edge

A notification is a miniature UI: with actions attached, the task completes without ever opening the app — which is the highest compliment a notification can pay.

“Mark watered” and “Snooze” ride on Sprout’s push; reply rides on a message. When the user does tap through, deep-link to the exact screen with back behaving correctly (12.6) — landing on a home screen after a specific push is a broken promise.

23.6

Badges must tell the truth

A badge is a count of things the user can actually resolve. A badge that won’t clear, counts marketing, or reads “1” forever trains the eye to ignore it — and the red dot’s power, once spent, is gone app-wide.

Badge only user-resolvable items, clear it the moment they’re seen, and let users turn it off per type. In-app announcements belong in an inbox surface, not on the app icon.

24

Internationalization

A layout that only works in English isn’t finished — it’s untested

Most of the world doesn’t read your source language, and some of it reads right-to-left. Internationalization isn’t translation — it’s designing so translation can’t break anything: elastic layouts, mirrored flows, and formats that come from the locale, not from the designer’s habits.

Same intent, four realities — design the container, not the string
24.1

Text expands — plan for +35%

German runs about a third longer than English; short strings can double; Finnish and Russian bring their own surprises. Every fixed-width label is a truncation waiting for a locale.

Buttons, tabs, and rows size themselves from their content with room to grow; text wraps or the container stretches — it never silently ellipsizes a verb (7.2’s rule, new cause). The one-word tab labels from 8.3 earn their keep here.

24.2

RTL mirrors the flow, not the world

In Arabic and Hebrew the reading direction reverses, and with it the layout: navigation chevrons, back gestures, progress bars, list alignments. But clocks, phone numbers, media-playback controls, and musical timelines stay put.

Think and build in leading/trailing, never left/right — then the mirror is free on both platforms. Audit icons for directionality: a “send” arrow flips; a checkmark doesn’t. Test with a real RTL locale, not just the debug flag.

24.3

Formats are locale data, not style choices

03/04/2026 is March in one country and April in another; “1,500” differs by a factor of a thousand across a border. Dates, numbers, currency, units, name order, and week-start are all facts about the locale.

Always format through the platform’s locale APIs — never hand-assemble a date string or hardcode a currency symbol. Sorting too: alphabetical order is locale-dependent, and “Å” files differently in Oslo and in Ohio.

24.4

Never build sentences from parts

“You have ” + count + “ plants” breaks the moment a language changes word order or has three plural forms — and many do.

Full-sentence templates with named placeholders, plural rules handled by the platform (ICU/stringsdict), and a context note for the translator on every ambiguous string (“Water” the verb, not the noun). Concatenation is how apps end up speaking broken everything.

24.5

Culture rides along with color and symbol

Red is danger in one market and celebration in another; an owl is wisdom or ill omen; hand gestures are a minefield; idioms (“home run”, “slam dunk”) simply don’t travel.

Keep source copy idiom-free and concrete (19.2 helps), don’t let color alone carry meaning (7.4 — now for a second reason), and have market reviewers sanity-check imagery before a launch, not after the screenshots go viral.

24.6

Pseudo-localize before you translate

One build with elongated accented strings (“Àððð þļåñţ~~~”) and forced RTL finds the truncations, clippings, and concatenations before a single translator is paid.

Run pseudo-localization as part of the screen audit (28) for any app going multilingual. And remember language ≠ region: a user can want English with metric units and euros — respect the system’s separate settings, and never guess locale from IP.

25

AI Interface Patterns

Probabilistic features need deterministic UX

AI features break two assumptions the rest of this manual rests on: the output isn’t predictable, and it can be confidently wrong. The interface’s job is to wrap that uncertainty in deterministic guarantees — clear provenance, easy verification, and an undo for everything. The laws still hold; the stakes just went up.

The autonomy ladder — reversible trifles run free; irreversible acts only suggest
25.1

Autonomy is earned per action, not per model

How capable the model is doesn’t matter; how costly a mistake is does. A spelling fix can run autonomously; filing and tagging act with undo; an email is drafted; money only ever gets suggested.

Classify every AI-touchable action by reversibility and blast radius, then pin it to a rung. Let users promote actions they’ve grown to trust — never promote silently on their behalf (3.3, at machine speed).

25.2

Streaming is a loading state — design it

Tokens arriving live beat a spinner (20’s ladder applies: generation usually lives in the 1–10s band), but streaming text is content that moves — the thing 20.3 forbids.

Reserve stable space for the response, keep actions pinned so nothing shifts under a poised finger, always show a Stop button, and cover pre-stream latency with a skeleton, not silence. When generation fails mid-stream, keep the partial and say so.

25.3

Uncertainty is content, not fine print

Models state guesses in the grammar of facts. The interface has to restore the difference — because the user can’t hear the model’s confidence, only its tone.

Cite sources inline and make them tappable; use calibrated language for inference (“Looks like overwatering” vs. “Your plant has root rot”); separate retrieved facts from generated prose visually. Verification must cost one tap, or nobody pays it.

25.4

Show the work, keep the receipt

When AI acts — renames, files, edits, replies — the user needs to see what changed and reverse it as one unit. Undo beats confirmation here more than anywhere (3.3), because confirmations would arrive constantly and get dismissed blindly.

Every AI action produces a visible, diff-style record (“Renamed 12 files — review · undo”); batch operations get a review screen before or a rollback after. An AI feature without undo is a trust incident on a timer.

25.5

Chat is the fallback, not the default

A blank prompt box is Hick’s law inverted — infinite options produce paralysis, then three-word prompts, then disappointment. Chat earns its place only where intent is genuinely open-ended.

For known intents, give buttons, chips, and structured inputs that invoke the model invisibly (“Diagnose this leaf” beats “describe your problem”). Where chat is right, seed it with tappable example prompts — recognition over recall (3.6) applies to talking, too.

25.6

Label the machine, own its mistakes

AI-generated content is labeled as such — not to cover the vendor, but because authorship is context users need. And when the model is wrong in your product, it’s your product that was wrong.

Mark generated content at the point of consumption, wire feedback controls to something real, and design the failure path with the same care as success (16.3): “I couldn’t tell from this photo — try one in daylight” beats a hallucinated diagnosis every single time.

26

What Not To Do

Universal sins, plus the don’ts Apple and Google spell out themselves

Anti-patterns are cheaper to memorize than principles — you can veto a bad idea in seconds. The platform-specific ones below come straight from the HIG and Material 3 documentation.

Universal — any platform
✕

Mystery-meat navigation

Label the icons. An unlabeled hamburger, kebab, or abstract glyph forces a guess on every visit; recognition beats recall (3.6).

✕

Gesture-only features

Every swipe, long-press, or shake needs a visible path to the same outcome. Gestures are accelerators, not doors.

✕

Login wall before value

Let people taste the product first. Ask for the account when it protects something of theirs, not before.

✕

Dark patterns

No confirmshaming (“No thanks, I hate saving money”), no hidden unsubscribe, no disguised ads, no pre-ticked upsells. They convert once and churn forever — and increasingly, they’re illegal.

✕

Disabled buttons with no explanation

Say what’s missing, next to the button, or keep it enabled and validate on tap with a precise message.

✕

Color as the only signal

Pair color with an icon, label, or weight change. Both Apple and Google call this out — it fails color-blind users and dark-mode edge cases alike.

✕

Fighting the system

No app-level light/dark toggle, no hijacked back gesture, no custom-drawn fake-native controls. The OS always wins; users side with the OS.

✕

Modal inside modal

A modal is a scoped task, not an app-within-the-app. One level, an obvious way out, and confirmation only when data would be lost.

iOS — Apple’s own don’ts
✕

Actions in the tab bar

Tabs are places, not verbs. Actions belong in toolbars. Never hide the tab bar during navigation, and avoid the More tab — fewer, better tabs.

✕

Custom bar backgrounds & tints

Liquid Glass adapts to the content beneath it; painting over it breaks the system look. Use the scroll edge effect for separation and keep bar icons monochromatic.

✕

Glass in the content layer

Liquid Glass is for the floating functional layer only. Content uses standard materials (ultraThin → thick) with vibrant label colors.

✕

Full-width and over-prominent buttons

Inset buttons from the margins, concentric with the hardware corners. At most two prominent buttons per view — and a destructive action is never the primary one.

✕

Stacked alerts and sheets

One modal at a time, one alert ever on screen. A “Back” text label instead of the chevron, or Done without Cancel, reads as broken to iOS hands.

Android — Google’s own don’ts
✕

Navigation drawers in new designs

M3 Expressive deprecates the drawer — use the expanded navigation rail. Bottom bar holds 3–5 destinations; more than that means the IA needs work, not a drawer.

✕

FAB misuse

One FAB, for the screen’s single most important constructive action. Never multiple, never minor, never destructive. Related sub-actions → FAB menu.

✕

Critical info in a snackbar

Snackbars vanish. Anything the user must see or decide gets a dialog; anything ongoing gets inline status. One snackbar at a time.

✕

Chips as buttons

Chips are dynamic and contextual (filters, suggestions, tokens); buttons are fixed CTAs. Swapping them confuses both roles.

✕

Hard-coded colors

Hex literals break dynamic color and dark theme in one stroke. Everything maps to a color role — that’s what makes Material You work.

✕

Expressive everywhere

Bounce, shape morphs, and emphasized type are for one or two hero moments. High-frequency, utilitarian flows use the Standard motion scheme.

The Honest Trade-offs

Where the pin sits by default — and what moves it

These axes have no correct answer, only a considered position. The pin marks the default stance; the note says what justifies moving it.

ConventionNovelty

Default: lean conventional. Novelty is a budget, not a virtue — spend it only on the interaction that defines the product. Everything else should be instantly familiar. Move right only when differentiation is the strategy and onboarding can carry the cost.

ClarityDensity

Default: clarity. Whitespace is not wasted space; it is structure. Move right for expert tools used hours daily — traders, dispatchers, IDE users — where re-finding information costs more than visual calm.

DiscoverabilityAesthetics

Default: discoverability. A beautiful interface no one can operate is a sculpture. Labels beat mystery-meat icons; visible affordances beat hidden gestures. Move right only after usage data proves the pattern is learned.

Guided flowUser freedom

Default: genuinely split. Guide first-run and high-stakes flows (payments, deletion, setup); free everything else. A wizard for a task users repeat daily becomes a cage by week two.

Speed to shipPolish

Default: ship, then polish the peaks. Per the peak–end rule, concentrate polish on the success moment and the ending. A uniformly 80%-polished app beats one perfect screen among six rough ones.

28

The Screen Audit

Run any screen through these twelve — click to check off

  1. One primary action? If two elements compete for “most important,” neither wins.
  2. Obvious in 5 seconds? A stranger can say what the screen is for and what to do next.
  3. Thumb test passed? Primary action reachable one-handed; destructive actions out of casual reach.
  4. Targets ≥ 44pt/48dp? Including the gaps between them.
  5. Every state designed? Empty, loading, error, partial, offline — not just the happy path.
  6. Feedback within 100 ms? Every tap acknowledged; anything over 1 s gets a progress indicator.
  7. Undo over confirm? Reversibility designed in before confirmation dialogs bolted on.
  8. Recognition over recall? No information carried between screens in the user’s head.
  9. Contrast & type accessible? 4.5:1 for body text; layout survives 200% text scaling.
  10. Keyboard respected? Focused field visible, right input type, submit reachable (mobile); full tab order (web/desktop).
  11. Platform dialect spoken? Back works the native way; controls feel at home on this OS.
  12. State survives interruption? Rotation, backgrounding, a phone call — nothing typed is lost.

A Worked Example

The whole manual, run once — “Sprout,” a plant-care tracker, from idea to two dialects

Theory earns its keep only when it survives contact with a real product. This chapter runs the chapter 08 process end to end on one small idea, exercising the laws, the hierarchy levers, and both dialect layers along the way.

29.1

Frame it — runs 8.1

“For apartment plant owners who forget care schedules, Sprout tracks watering and feeding — and it is brilliant at answering what needs me today?”

That last clause already made the big decisions: the home screen is a task list, not a plant gallery; the hero datum is today’s tasks, not the collection. Every dispute below resolves against this sentence.

29.2

Map the flows — runs 8.2

Three journeys: the daily loop (check & mark done), setup (add a plant), and the occasional rescue (diagnose a sad plant). The daily loop is the 80% flow, so it gets the step budget.

Two taps from open to done — that’s the budget to protect
29.3

Choose the IA — runs 8.3

Three peer destinations, each one word: Today (the loop), Plants (the collection), Learn (the rescue guides). Tab/nav-bar model on both platforms.

“Add plant” is frequent-enough, not the hero — the hero is marking tasks done, which lives inline on the rows (Fitts: zero travel). Adding gets the platform’s secondary slot: a toolbar + on iOS, a FAB on Android — the first visible dialect fork.

29.4

Wireframe the core loop — runs 8.4

One grayscale, platform-neutral pass with real content — real plant names, real task verbs.

No color, no dialect — if hierarchy fails here, no theme rescues it (5.6)
29.5

Split into dialects — runs 8.5

Same core — same three rows, same words, same reading order — expressed twice. Every visible difference below is a chapter 11 table row, not a whim.

iOS — large title · inset list · toolbar “+” · floating tab bar
Android — app bar · list + avatars · FAB · nav bar with active pill

Identical: IA, row order, words, the ice-blue accent, the two-tap loop. Forked: title treatment (large title vs. app bar), add-plant entry point (toolbar + vs. FAB), check affordance (circle vs. checkbox), list anatomy (inset group vs. avatar rows), tab bar (floating pill vs. full-width with active pill). Marking done confirms with a haptic tick on iOS, a snackbar with Undo on Android.

29.6

Design the edges — runs 8.6

The two edge states that matter most here: the success-empty state (all plants tended — Today’s most common state in a healthy habit) and the notification permission ask.

Empty Today celebrates (“All watered — see you Thursday”) and offers the next likely intent (browse Plants), never a dead end. The permission ask waits until the user adds their first plant and is primed in context — “Want a nudge when Monstera is thirsty?” — because an OS permission dialog at cold launch is answered no out of pure self-defense.

29.7

Audit & iterate — runs 8.7

Running the chapter 28 checklist against the mockups caught three real issues before any polish was spent.

① “Delete plant” initially sat as a swipe action on Today rows — destructive within thumb reach of the daily tap; moved into the plant detail screen behind a confirm. ② Check circles were 32pt — below target; hit areas padded to 44/48 while glyphs stayed small. ③ The empty state failed the 5-second test (a blank list reads as “broken”) and gained its celebratory copy. Then back to 8.4 with the findings.
