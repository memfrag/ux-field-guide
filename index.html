01

Working Posture

How every design question gets approached before any pixel moves

Principles are worthless without a posture — a default stance that decides what to reach for first. These four govern everything below.

☝

Mobile-first instincts

Thumb reach, one-handed use, and platform conventions come before layout aesthetics. If it fails on a phone held in one hand on a moving train, it fails.

→

Opinionated, not neutral

A recommendation with reasoning beats a survey of options. “It depends” is only acceptable when immediately followed by what it depends on and which way to lean.

§

Grounded in the classics

Nielsen, Fitts, Hick, gestalt — applied pragmatically, not recited. Theory earns its place only when it changes a decision.

⇄

Honest about trade-offs

Every design choice buys something and pays for it. Name both sides. A critique that lists only benefits is marketing, not design.

02

Laws of Interaction

The physics of interfaces — they apply whether you believe in them or not

2.1

Fitts’s Law

Time to acquire a target is a function of distance to and size of the target. Small, far-away targets are slow and error-prone.

Primary actions get big targets near the thumb or cursor rest position. Screen edges and corners are effectively infinite-size targets on desktop — use them.

2.2

Hick’s Law

Decision time grows with the number and complexity of choices. Every added option taxes every user, every time.

One primary action per screen. Cut, bury, or stage everything else via progressive disclosure. A menu of 5 beats a menu of 12, even for power users.

2.3

Jakob’s Law

Users spend most of their time in other apps. They arrive with expectations formed elsewhere and your interface inherits them.

Deviate from convention only where your product’s core differentiation lives. Spend novelty budget on the one thing that matters; keep everything else boring.

2.4

Miller’s Limit

Working memory holds roughly 4–7 chunks. Interfaces that require remembering state across screens are leaking cognition.

Recognition over recall: show the selected plan, the applied filter, the cart total. Never make the user carry information from one screen to the next in their head.

2.5

Tesler’s Law

Every process has irreducible complexity. It can be moved — to the user or into the system — but not deleted.

Absorb complexity into defaults, smart detection, and sensible presets. Ask the user only what the system genuinely cannot know.

2.6

Peak–End Rule

Experiences are judged by their most intense moment and their ending — not the average.

Polish the success state, the confirmation, the empty state after completing a task. A rough middle is forgivable; a rough ending is remembered.

03

Nielsen’s Ten, Condensed

The evaluation checklist — every screen answers to these

3.1

Visibility of system status

Every action gets feedback within 100 ms. Loading states, progress, confirmation. Silence is a bug.

3.2

Match the real world

Speak the user’s vocabulary, not the schema’s. “Trip” not “itinerary_record”.

3.3

User control & freedom

Undo beats confirmation dialogs. Every wizard needs an exit. Every destructive action needs a way back.

3.4

Consistency & standards

Internal consistency first, platform convention second, industry pattern third. Break ties in that order.

3.5

Error prevention

Disable the invalid, constrain the input, confirm the irreversible. Design so the error can’t happen, then handle it anyway.

3.6

Recognition over recall

Show options, history, and context inline. The interface remembers so the user doesn’t have to.

3.7

Flexibility & efficiency

Accelerators for experts — shortcuts, gestures, recents — that stay invisible to novices.

3.8

Aesthetic & minimalist design

Every element competes with every other element. If it doesn’t serve the task, it costs the task.

3.9

Help users recover from errors

Error messages state what happened, why, and the next step — in plain language, near the problem.

3.10

Help & documentation

The best help is contextual and just-in-time. If users need the manual, the screen has already failed once.

04

Gestalt Principles

How the eye groups before the mind reads

Users perceive structure before they read a single word. Spacing, alignment, and enclosure are doing information architecture whether you intend it or not.

Proximity
Similarity
Common region
Continuity
Figure / ground
Closure
4.1

Proximity

Whitespace is the strongest grouping tool you have. Related items sit closer together than unrelated ones — labels hug their fields, not the field above.

4.2

Similarity

Things that look alike are assumed to behave alike. If two buttons look the same, they’d better be the same weight of action.

4.3

Common region

A border or background groups more strongly than proximity. Use cards sparingly — every enclosure adds visual weight and fragments scanning.

4.4

Continuity & alignment

The eye follows lines. A single strong left-alignment edge scans faster than centered fragments. Break alignment only to signal a break in meaning.

4.5

Figure / ground

Contrast and elevation decide what reads as content and what reads as chrome. Modals, sheets, and scrims work by manipulating this deliberately.

4.6

Closure

A partially visible card at the screen edge is the strongest scroll affordance there is — stronger than any arrow or dot indicator.

05

Visual Hierarchy

The order the eye receives things is a design decision — make it on purpose

Every screen is read in some order. Hierarchy is choosing that order: what is seen first, second, third — and what is barely seen at all. When everything is emphasized, nothing is; emphasis is a zero-sum budget.

Flat — five things shout equally
Hierarchy — 1 what · 2 cost · 3 act
5.1

Size

The largest element is read first — no exceptions. A type scale exists to make this cheap and consistent.

One hero size per screen. The platform scales encode this already: Large Title 34pt over Body 17pt on iOS; Headline L 32sp over Body L 16sp in Material. If two elements tie for biggest, the screen has no first sentence.

5.2

Weight & contrast

Bold and high-contrast advances; light and low-contrast recedes. This lever is stronger than size at equal sizes.

De-emphasize with gray before you shrink — a 13pt secondary-gray label beats a 10pt black one for both hierarchy and legibility. That’s exactly what secondaryLabel (iOS) and onSurfaceVariant (M3) are for.

5.3

Space

Whitespace confers importance — margin is a spotlight. Crowding levels everything to the same rank.

Give the primary element roughly twice the breathing room of anything secondary. Spacing is also grouping (proximity, 4.1), so it does hierarchy and structure with one move.

5.4

Position

Scanning starts top-leading and follows an F or Z path; the thumb finishes at the bottom.

Message at the top, action at the bottom. First position in any list is the cheapest emphasis available — spend it on the most likely choice, not the alphabetically first one.

5.5

Color — the accent budget

On a restrained canvas, the accent color means “this matters, this is interactive.” Spend it like money.

Tint the one primary action and the key datum — nothing decorative. Both platforms enforce this: HIG wants monochromatic bars with a single tinted action; M3 routes emphasis through the primary role. This page does it too — one ice-blue, everywhere it counts.

5.6

The squint test

The verification step: squint at the screen (or blur a screenshot) until the words disappear.

Three levels should survive the blur: one hero, a support layer, and a whisper of detail. If the primary action doesn’t survive, the hierarchy failed — no amount of polish rescues it.

Same rule for actions — one filled · one outlined · text for the rest
06

Mobile Instincts

The phone is held, not sat at — design for the hand first

Roughly half of phone use is one-handed. The bottom half of the screen belongs to the thumb; the top corners are hostile territory reserved for rare actions.

Hard · rare actions Stretch · secondary Natural · primary

Rules of the hand

  • Primary actions live at the bottom. Tab bars, FABs, and sticky CTAs exist because of this map.
  • Destructive actions go out of easy reach — or behind a deliberate gesture. Reachability cuts both ways.
  • Top-left back button is a stretch. Support the edge-swipe gesture; never make the corner the only way home.
  • Reachability degrades with screen size. What was “natural” on 5.4″ is “stretch” on 6.7″.
✕28px — misses taps
✓44pt — HIG minimum
✓48dp — Material min.
6.1

Progressive disclosure over density

Show the 20% that serves 80% of visits; stage the rest behind clear, honest entry points. Density is a desktop luxury.

6.2

Gestures need visible fallbacks

Swipe-to-delete, pull-to-refresh, long-press: fine as accelerators, never as the only path. Undiscoverable ≠ elegant.

6.3

Respect the keyboard

The keyboard eats half the screen. Keep the focused field and the submit action visible above it; use the right keyboard type per field.

6.4

Design the interruptions

Mobile sessions are 30-second fragments. Preserve state ruthlessly — a form that loses input to a phone call has failed.

07

The Process

From app idea to shipped design — in order, no skipping

Screens are the last thing you draw, not the first. This sequence front-loads the decisions that are expensive to change (what it is, how it’s organized) and defers the ones that are cheap (colors, corner radii).

7.1

Frame the product

Who is it for, what job does it do, and what is the one thing it must do brilliantly. One sentence, written down.

“For [person] in [situation], this app [does job] — and it is brilliant at [one thing].” Every later design dispute gets resolved against this sentence.

7.2

Map the flows

The top three user journeys as task flows — before any screen exists.

Boxes are user intentions (“capture expense”, “find last invoice”), not screens. Count the steps in the most frequent flow; that number is your budget to beat.

7.3

Choose the information architecture

What lives where, and which navigation model carries it. The most expensive decision in the whole process.

2–5 peer destinations → tab/nav bar. One dominant feed plus actions → single screen with modals. Deep hierarchy → drill-down list. If you can’t name a tab in one word, it isn’t a tab.

7.4

Wireframe the core loop

Low fidelity, grayscale, one platform-neutral pass over the 20% of screens that serve 80% of sessions.

Real content, never lorem ipsum — layout decisions made against fake content are fake decisions. If the hierarchy needs color to work, the hierarchy is broken.

7.5

Split into dialects

One shared core — IA, flows, vocabulary, brand — expressed through two idiomatic layers: HIG for iOS, Material 3 for Android.

The user should recognize the app across platforms but never feel it was ported. Chapter 09 is the mapping table.

7.6

Design the edges

Empty, loading, error, offline, interrupted, first-run. The happy path is a minority of real sessions.

The empty state is the first screen most users ever meet — make it teach. An error state that only says “something went wrong” is a design task abandoned, not completed.

7.7

Audit, test, iterate

Run the screen audit (ch. 15), the thumb map (ch. 06), and put it in front of real people.

Five users surface most usability problems — test the wireframe, not the polished mockup, so findings are cheap to act on. Then loop back to 7.4.

08

Four Modes of Work

Every design engagement is one of these — know which one you’re in

Misdiagnosing the mode wastes the session: you can’t wireframe your way out of a strategy question, and a philosophical debate won’t fix a broken checkout. Name the mode, then run its playbook.

◎

Critique a project

A screen, flow, or product exists and needs expert eyes.

  1. Ask the goal and audience before judging anything
  2. Five-second test: what reads first, what’s the assumed action?
  3. Walk Nielsen’s ten (ch. 03) against each screen
  4. Thumb map + platform dialect audit (ch. 06, 09)
  5. Rank findings by severity × effort to fix

Every criticism ships with a concrete recommendation.

⌖

Diagnose a problem

Something feels off — confusion, drop-off, low conversion.

  1. Locate the drop-off precisely: which step, which screen
  2. Hypothesize against the laws — choice overload (Hick)? missing feedback (3.1)? recall burden (2.4)?
  3. Watch three real sessions before changing anything
  4. Fix the smallest plausible cause first; measure; repeat

Symptoms lie; funnels and session recordings don’t.

§

Reason a decision

“Should we do X or Y?” — a principle question in disguise.

  1. Name the trade-off axis it sits on (ch. 14)
  2. Find the governing law or heuristic
  3. Check three best-in-class apps — Jakob’s law sets expectations
  4. Decide, and write the reasoning down

A decision without written reasoning gets relitigated forever.

✎

Produce artifacts

Wireframes, mockups, prototypes — something to look at and test.

  1. Match fidelity to the question being asked
  2. Sketch answers flow · wireframe answers layout · mockup answers style · prototype answers feel
  3. Real content from the first sketch
  4. Derive both dialects from the shared core (7.5)

Never polish what hasn’t been validated one fidelity below.

09

One Design, Two Dialects

HIG for iOS · Material 3 for Android — same app, native accent

Cross-platform consistency matters less than platform fluency. Users compare your app to the other apps on their phone, not to your app on the other platform. Keep the core identical; translate the expression.

9.1

The iOS accent, in one breath

Controls float above content on Liquid Glass; the content owns the screen.

No custom bar backgrounds or tints — use the scroll edge effect. Monochromatic bar icons, one tinted prominent action. Large titles that collapse on scroll. Sheets with medium/large detents for scoped tasks. Semantic colors, both appearances, always.

9.2

The Android accent, in one breath

Build on tokens — color roles, type scale, shape scale — so the app inherits Material You personalization for free.

Nav bar → rail as the window widens. The FAB carries the action. Bottom sheets over menus on compact. Predictive back everywhere. Save expressive moves — springs, shape morphs, emphasized type — for one or two hero moments.

9.3

Desktop is not a big phone

Density, hover, multi-window, drag-and-drop, and keyboard-first flows are desktop’s native strengths. A stretched mobile layout on a 27″ display wastes the medium.

9.4

Web is the ambiguous middle

Assume both touch and cursor. Deep-linkable URLs, working browser back, and responsive reflow are the web’s conventions — break them and the platform breaks you.

10.1

Same paradigm, different gifts

Both are declarative trees re-rendered from state. What differs is the free lunch: SwiftUI gives platform behavior implicitly — Dynamic Type, edge-swipe back, Liquid Glass, dark mode. Compose gives Material behavior explicitly through Scaffold slots — snackbars, FAB placement, predictive back, dynamic color.

Take the lunch. Screens assembled from built-ins inherit each platform’s next redesign for free; custom-drawn screens are frozen at the year you shipped them.

10.2

Styling vs. slots

SwiftUI customizes by restyling semantics — one Button, many ButtonStyles. Compose customizes by filling slots — distinct composables per emphasis level, each with content lambdas.

In both, restyle before you rebuild: a restyled built-in keeps its accessibility, press states, haptics, and focus behavior. A rebuilt lookalike keeps none and you re-earn each one by hand.

10.3

The gaps run in opposite directions — on purpose

SwiftUI has no FAB and no snackbar; Compose has no stepper and no grouped-list style. These aren’t omissions — they’re the dialects (ch. 09) enforced at the API level.

Reaching for a component the framework refuses to provide is a design smell: you’re usually importing the other platform’s idiom. Check the intent row in the table above and use the native answer instead.

10.4

One codebase means one dialect

Compose Multiplatform renders Material on iPhones; Flutter renders Flutter everywhere. Write-once UI buys speed by giving every user the same dialect — which is precisely what chapter 09 spends itself avoiding.

A legitimate trade for tools, prototypes, and brand-heavy apps where the product is the interface everywhere. Make it as a decision (ch. 14), not as a default — and know Jakob’s law bills iOS users for Material controls either way.

11

Design Systems

Decisions made once, so screens don’t relitigate them

A design system is the product’s design memory: every choice that shouldn’t be re-made per screen — captured as tokens, components, patterns, and the rules for using them. Its value isn’t beauty; it’s that a hundred screens stay coherent while ten people ship them.

Change flows upward — fix the token, and every layer above inherits it
11.1

A system is rules, not a UI kit

A Figma library without usage guidance is a parts bin. The system is the components plus when-to-use, when-not-to, and who decides.

Every component ships with its anti-patterns: “segmented control — 2–5 options, never 1” is the documentation format. If a component needs a meeting to use correctly, the docs failed.

11.2

Start from the platform’s system

HIG components and Material 3 are mature, accessible, user-tested design systems you get for free — with dark mode, Dynamic Type, and predictive back already solved.

Wrap and theme system components; rebuild only where the product’s differentiation lives (Jakob’s law economics, 2.3). Every custom control is a maintenance contract with no expiry date.

11.3

Detach rate is the health metric

When designers keep overriding a component or spawning one-off variants, that isn’t indiscipline — it’s the system telling you a decision is wrong or missing.

Audit the one-offs quarterly. Three teams hacking the same card variant means the system owes them that variant; promote it, don’t police them.

11.4

One system, two dialects

The system mirrors chapter 09: a shared semantic layer (tokens, naming, patterns) with per-platform component implementations.

One token sheet feeds both platforms; “primary action” resolves to a prominent toolbar button on iOS and a FAB or filled button on Android. Share the decision, not the pixels.

11.5

Governance or decay — pick one

A system nobody can change becomes a cage; a system anybody can change becomes noise. Both end with teams ignoring it.

Name an owner. Provide a lightweight contribution path (propose → review → promote). Version it, deprecate loudly, and never break existing screens silently.

12

Design Tokens

Name the decision, not the value — the value will change

A token is a named design decision: color.action.primary, not #4cc2ff. The name captures intent; the value is an implementation detail that can be swapped per theme, platform, or brand without touching a single screen.

Components reference semantic tokens — never primitives directly
12.1

Three tiers, strict direction

Primitives hold raw values (blue-500, space-4). Semantic tokens give them meaning (color.accent, space.inset.card). Component tokens bind them to parts (button.fill).

References flow one way: component → semantic → primitive. A screen that reaches past the semantic layer to grab blue-500 has re-made a decision the system already made.

12.2

Name by role, never by value

If the name would be wrong after the value changes, the name is wrong now. accent survives a rebrand; blue doesn’t.

The platforms are already role-named token systems — secondaryLabel and semantic colors on iOS, primary/onSurfaceVariant color roles and the type scale in M3. Map your brand onto those roles rather than inventing a parallel vocabulary.

12.3

Tokens make theming free

Dark mode, Material You dynamic color, high-contrast, density, white-labeling — each is just a different value set behind the same names.

This page is the demo: it went paper-and-vermilion → carbon → graphite-and-ice-blue by editing a handful of CSS variables, zero markup changes. If a retheme requires touching screens, tokens were bypassed somewhere.

12.4

Tokenize more than color

Type styles, the spacing scale, corner radii, elevation, motion durations and springs, z-order — anything decided more than once deserves a name.

Spacing on a 4pt/4dp base scale; radii mapped to the platform shape scale (M3: none → full); motion as tokens (M3’s spatial/effects springs, fast/default/slow) so “how things move” is one decision, not fifty.

13

What Not To Do

Universal sins, plus the don’ts Apple and Google spell out themselves

Anti-patterns are cheaper to memorize than principles — you can veto a bad idea in seconds. The platform-specific ones below come straight from the HIG and Material 3 documentation.

Universal — any platform
✕

Mystery-meat navigation

Label the icons. An unlabeled hamburger, kebab, or abstract glyph forces a guess on every visit; recognition beats recall (3.6).

✕

Gesture-only features

Every swipe, long-press, or shake needs a visible path to the same outcome. Gestures are accelerators, not doors.

✕

Login wall before value

Let people taste the product first. Ask for the account when it protects something of theirs, not before.

✕

Dark patterns

No confirmshaming (“No thanks, I hate saving money”), no hidden unsubscribe, no disguised ads, no pre-ticked upsells. They convert once and churn forever — and increasingly, they’re illegal.

✕

Disabled buttons with no explanation

Say what’s missing, next to the button, or keep it enabled and validate on tap with a precise message.

✕

Color as the only signal

Pair color with an icon, label, or weight change. Both Apple and Google call this out — it fails color-blind users and dark-mode edge cases alike.

✕

Fighting the system

No app-level light/dark toggle, no hijacked back gesture, no custom-drawn fake-native controls. The OS always wins; users side with the OS.

✕

Modal inside modal

A modal is a scoped task, not an app-within-the-app. One level, an obvious way out, and confirmation only when data would be lost.

iOS — Apple’s own don’ts
✕

Actions in the tab bar

Tabs are places, not verbs. Actions belong in toolbars. Never hide the tab bar during navigation, and avoid the More tab — fewer, better tabs.

✕

Custom bar backgrounds & tints

Liquid Glass adapts to the content beneath it; painting over it breaks the system look. Use the scroll edge effect for separation and keep bar icons monochromatic.

✕

Glass in the content layer

Liquid Glass is for the floating functional layer only. Content uses standard materials (ultraThin → thick) with vibrant label colors.

✕

Full-width and over-prominent buttons

Inset buttons from the margins, concentric with the hardware corners. At most two prominent buttons per view — and a destructive action is never the primary one.

✕

Stacked alerts and sheets

One modal at a time, one alert ever on screen. A “Back” text label instead of the chevron, or Done without Cancel, reads as broken to iOS hands.

Android — Google’s own don’ts
✕

Navigation drawers in new designs

M3 Expressive deprecates the drawer — use the expanded navigation rail. Bottom bar holds 3–5 destinations; more than that means the IA needs work, not a drawer.

✕

FAB misuse

One FAB, for the screen’s single most important constructive action. Never multiple, never minor, never destructive. Related sub-actions → FAB menu.

✕

Critical info in a snackbar

Snackbars vanish. Anything the user must see or decide gets a dialog; anything ongoing gets inline status. One snackbar at a time.

✕

Chips as buttons

Chips are dynamic and contextual (filters, suggestions, tokens); buttons are fixed CTAs. Swapping them confuses both roles.

✕

Hard-coded colors

Hex literals break dynamic color and dark theme in one stroke. Everything maps to a color role — that’s what makes Material You work.

✕

Expressive everywhere

Bounce, shape morphs, and emphasized type are for one or two hero moments. High-frequency, utilitarian flows use the Standard motion scheme.

14

The Honest Trade-offs

Where the pin sits by default — and what moves it

These axes have no correct answer, only a considered position. The pin marks the default stance; the note says what justifies moving it.

ConventionNovelty

Default: lean conventional. Novelty is a budget, not a virtue — spend it only on the interaction that defines the product. Everything else should be instantly familiar. Move right only when differentiation is the strategy and onboarding can carry the cost.

ClarityDensity

Default: clarity. Whitespace is not wasted space; it is structure. Move right for expert tools used hours daily — traders, dispatchers, IDE users — where re-finding information costs more than visual calm.

DiscoverabilityAesthetics

Default: discoverability. A beautiful interface no one can operate is a sculpture. Labels beat mystery-meat icons; visible affordances beat hidden gestures. Move right only after usage data proves the pattern is learned.

Guided flowUser freedom

Default: genuinely split. Guide first-run and high-stakes flows (payments, deletion, setup); free everything else. A wizard for a task users repeat daily becomes a cage by week two.

Speed to shipPolish

Default: ship, then polish the peaks. Per the peak–end rule, concentrate polish on the success moment and the ending. A uniformly 80%-polished app beats one perfect screen among six rough ones.

  3. Thumb test passed? Primary action reachable one-handed; destructive actions out of casual reach.
  4. Targets ≥ 44pt/48dp? Including the gaps between them.
  5. Every state designed? Empty, loading, error, partial, offline — not just the happy path.
  6. Feedback within 100 ms? Every tap acknowledged; anything over 1 s gets a progress indicator.
  7. Undo over confirm? Reversibility designed in before confirmation dialogs bolted on.
  8. Recognition over recall? No information carried between screens in the user’s head.
  9. Contrast & type accessible? 4.5:1 for body text; layout survives 200% text scaling.
  10. Keyboard respected? Focused field visible, right input type, submit reachable (mobile); full tab order (web/desktop).
  11. Platform dialect spoken? Back works the native way; controls feel at home on this OS.
  12. State survives interruption? Rotation, backgrounding, a phone call — nothing typed is lost.
